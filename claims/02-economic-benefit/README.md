# Claim 02 — "Connecticut didn't benefit" from expanding natural gas

**CCEA report:** *How Did We Get Here?* (CCEA / Fred Carstensen)

> "…the well-intended expansion of generation capacity … delivered no reduction
> in rates, and **generated no significant benefits to Connecticut's economy**."
> — Executive Summary

The report supports this with a correlation — Connecticut expanded gas
generation, and the state's economy and payrolls stayed flat — and contrasts
Connecticut with Massachusetts' "most dynamic economy."

## Why this one is different

Unlike the emissions and cost claims, this is **not an empirical finding** — it
is an interpretation resting on an unanalyzed correlation. There is no mechanism
linking the gas expansion to economic performance, no counterfactual for what
the economy would have done otherwise, and no attempt to control for any other
variable. It can't be "refuted" with a better statistic because it was never
established with one. It can only be shown to lack analytical rigor — which it
does, in two distinct ways.

## Prong 1 — the method proves too much

The argument's form is: *the state adopted X, total payrolls stayed flat,
therefore X did not benefit the state.* That test would erase the value of
nearly every transformative technology in history. By the identical logic:

- broadband and the internet did nothing for Connecticut,
- the interstate highway system did nothing for Connecticut,
- electrification did nothing for Connecticut.

Each coincided with flat payrolls; none had its benefit show up as headcount in
its own sector. Flat aggregate employment simply cannot measure what a
capital-intensive input contributes — its value is the cheaper, better service
it gives every *other* part of the economy. A test that "disproves" the internet
is not a test.

## Prong 2 — even granting the correlation, it points nowhere

Set the method aside and take the correlation at face value: it is still
underdetermined. Connecticut's sluggish growth has many well-documented
candidate explanations that have nothing to do with electricity generation —
among them an economy heavily weighted toward finance and insurance, one of the
slowest post-2008 recoveries in the country, an aging population and net
outmigration, a high cost of living, and the departure of major corporate
headquarters. Any of these could plausibly bear on payrolls. CCEA isolates none
of them, yet assigns the outcome to energy policy.

## Verdict

The claim has **neither a valid method nor an isolated cause.** Prong 1 shows
the instrument is broken; Prong 2 shows that even if it worked, the reading
would be ambiguous. "Connecticut didn't benefit" is an opinion presented as a
finding — and the burden of proof rests with whoever asserts the causal link,
which the report never discharges.

> Note on scope: this entry deliberately offers **no counter-statistic**.
> Answering a bare correlation with another bare correlation would repeat the
> same error in reverse. The point is that the claim does not clear the bar to
> be treated as evidence. The measurable benefits of the transition — rates,
> reliability, and emissions — are assessed in the data-driven claims.
